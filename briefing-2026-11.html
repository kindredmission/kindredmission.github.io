Payments Compliance Weather · November 2026

The forecast for payments compliance

A record U.S. anti-money laundering penalty, the FTC’s continued focus on payment processors, and card network rules now in force for recurring payments and dispute monitoring.

Cloudy

Card network rules

Mastercard’s recurring-payment linking rule is now live, and Visa’s lower merchant threshold has applied since April.

October 23, 2026Global

Mastercard now requires the original Transaction Link ID on merchant-initiated charges

Since June 2, 2026, Mastercard has required processors to retain the Transaction Link ID (TLID) from the cardholder-initiated transaction that sets up future card use. From October 23, 2026, processors must send that TLID with every later merchant-initiated transaction, such as recurring or subscription charges. Network transaction IDs continue to be used alongside it.

Why it matters

Merchants that store cards outside their processor, or that migrated stored cards, are the most likely to be missing the original identifier.

What to do

Confirm with your processor how TLIDs are stored and sent, and how cards saved before June 2 are handled.

Sources

  • Stripe documentation: Mastercard Transaction Link ID

April 1, 2026Global, excluding MENA

Visa’s lower VAMP threshold for merchants is in force

Visa’s Acquirer Monitoring Program (VAMP) counts fraud reports and disputes in a single ratio. From April 1, 2026, the excessive threshold for merchants fell from 2.2% to 1.5%. Acquirer thresholds are 0.5% (above standard) and 0.7% (excessive). Merchant thresholds apply once a merchant has more than 1,500 combined fraud reports and disputes.

Why it matters

In aggregated setups, such as merchants of record and PayFacs, one seller can drive the ratio while the overall account still looks healthy.

What to do

Track fraud reports and disputes per seller, not just per account.

Sources

  • Checkout.com: Update to the Visa Acquirer Monitoring Program

Cloudy

Regulatory priorities

Regulators are focusing on payment intermediaries and on national security risks in AML programs.

September 2026United States

FTC leadership sets out a knowledge standard for processor liability

Alongside the Nuvei settlement, FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson and Commissioner Mark Meador issued a joint statement on when a payment processor can be liable for unfair practices. As summarized by Greenberg Traurig, the standard requires that the processor knew, should have known, or consciously avoided knowing that a merchant was engaged in unlawful conduct.

Why it matters

Screening and monitoring protect a processor only when red flags lead to action. Unanswered warning signs are where exposure grows.

What to do

Check that complaints, alerts and other providers’ terminations lead to documented decisions.

Sources

  • FTC press release, Sep 4, 2026
  • Greenberg Traurig analysis, Oct 15, 2026

October 13, 2026United States

FinCEN ties AML failures to national security priorities

Announcing its Bank of America penalty, FinCEN described the action as addressing the bank’s failure to mitigate and report priority national security risks, including Chinese money laundering networks and illicit finance along the southwest border.

Why it matters

Supervisors are judging programs on whether they detect the risks that national priorities name, not only on whether controls exist.

What to do

Map your monitoring scenarios to FinCEN’s published priorities.

Sources

  • FinCEN press release, Oct 13, 2026

Stormy

Regulatory enforcement

A $1.2 billion FinCEN penalty, and the FTC keeps acting against payment processors.

November 2, 2026United States

FTC and Washington act against a payment processor over small-business sales practices

The FTC and the State of Washington alleged that Riverside Payments, its affiliate Cascade Equipment Leasing and their operators misled small businesses with savings claims, incomplete quotes, buried auto-renewal and early termination terms, and a hard-to-use cancellation guarantee. The stipulated order requires a $600,000 payment, with a $32.1 million judgment suspended, and requires clear disclosure, express consent for auto-renewals and a simple way to cancel. These are allegations.

Why it matters

Deception and auto-renewal principles are being applied to how processors sell to businesses, not only to consumers.

What to do

Review merchant-facing quotes, savings claims, renewal terms and cancellation flows.

Sources

  • FTC press release, Nov 2, 2026

October 13, 2026United States

Bank of America: $1.2 billion FinCEN penalty, with OCC and OFAC actions

FinCEN assessed a $1.2 billion penalty against Bank of America, N.A. The bank admitted willful Bank Secrecy Act violations, including failing to maintain an adequate AML program, to conduct due diligence on foreign correspondent accounts and to file required Suspicious Activity Reports. The OCC assessed a $750 million civil money penalty, and OFAC announced an $11.6 million settlement over apparent sanctions violations.

Why it matters

Coordinated actions by several agencies raise the cost of a single program failure.

What to do

Test whether your monitoring would catch, and report, the patterns named in the action.

Sources

  • FinCEN press release, Oct 13, 2026

September 4, 2026United States

Nuvei settles FTC claims over processing for deceptive merchants

The FTC alleged that Nuvei opened and kept accounts for merchants it knew or should have known were engaged in deception, including more than $30 million in payments for the Reimage tech support scheme. The order requires $4.85 million for consumer redress, bans processing for telemarketed or pop-up tech support sellers and tactics such as load balancing, and requires screening and monitoring of clients.

Why it matters

The case sits alongside the FTC’s 2025 action against Paddle, which also involved Reimage.

What to do

Check your controls against load balancing, terminated-merchant re-entry and chargeback-driven escalation.

Sources

  • FTC press release, Sep 4, 2026
