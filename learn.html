Human trafficking

Using force, fraud or coercion to make someone work or provide commercial sex for someone else’s profit. It does not require crossing a border or being locked up, and for anyone under 18, commercial sex is trafficking even without force.

Myths and facts

Select a statement to see what U.S. federal law says.

MythTrafficking means moving someone from one place to another.

FactMoving a person is one possible action, not a requirement. Recruiting, harboring, providing or obtaining a person can be enough. Someone can be trafficked without ever leaving their home, workplace or city.

MythIt only happens across international borders.

FactNo border crossing is required. Trafficking can take place entirely within one house, one business or one town.

MythTrafficking and smuggling are the same thing.

FactThey are different crimes. Smuggling generally concerns helping someone cross a border without authorization. Trafficking concerns exploitation. The two can overlap, but one does not imply the other.

MythIf there is no physical violence, it isn’t trafficking.

FactForce is only one of three possible means. Fraud, such as lies about a job, pay or a relationship, or coercion, such as threats, a debt that never shrinks or threats of deportation, can meet the legal standard on its own.

MythIf the person agreed to it at first, it can’t be trafficking.

FactAgreeing at the start does not authorize what comes after. Accepting a job, a trip or a relationship is not consent to being forced, deceived or pressured into exploitation later.

MythA minor who seems willing is making a choice.

FactUnder U.S. federal law, a commercial sex act involving anyone under 18 is a severe form of sex trafficking. Force, fraud or coercion does not have to be proven.

MythAny abuse or disappearance is probably trafficking.

FactAssault, abduction and homicide are serious crimes that can happen without the legal elements of trafficking. Using accurate terms leads to better data and better help.

Three questions to ask

Most situations can be understood through three questions. This is a practical aid, not a substitute for the statute or the facts of a specific case.

QuestionWhat it covers
Action: what was done?Recruiting, harboring, transporting, providing, obtaining, patronizing or soliciting a person.
Means: how was the person controlled?Force, fraud or coercion, including threats, lies, manipulated debt and abuse of the legal process.
Purpose: to what end?A commercial sex act, or labor or services under involuntary servitude, peonage, debt bondage or slavery.

When the person is under 18: for a minor in commercial sexual exploitation, the “means” question drops out. Force, fraud or coercion does not have to be shown.

What the law says

The core U.S. definitions are in the Trafficking Victims Protection Act of 2000, as amended, at 22 U.S.C. § 7102.

Sex trafficking

Recruiting, harboring, transporting, providing, obtaining, patronizing or soliciting a person for the purpose of a commercial sex act. 22 U.S.C. § 7102(12)

It is a severe form of trafficking when the commercial sex act is induced by force, fraud or coercion, or when the person induced to perform it is under 18. § 7102(11)(A)

Labor trafficking

Recruiting, harboring, transporting, providing or obtaining a person for labor or services through force, fraud or coercion, for the purpose of involuntary servitude, peonage, debt bondage or slavery. § 7102(11)(B)

Physical restraint is not required. Threats, manipulated debt, psychological coercion and abuse of the legal process can all be relevant.

What “commercial” means

A commercial sex act is any sex act for which anything of value is given to or received by any person. It does not have to be cash. Food, shelter or drugs can count. § 7102(4)

The federal crimes

The definitions above come from the Trafficking Victims Protection Act. The federal criminal offenses carry additional elements, such as what the defendant knew:

  • 18 U.S.C. § 1591: sex trafficking of children, or by force, fraud or coercion
  • 18 U.S.C. § 1589: forced labor
  • 18 U.S.C. § 1590: trafficking with respect to peonage, slavery, involuntary servitude or forced labor

Warning signs

Look for signs of control. A person may:

  • not be free to come and go, or always be accompanied
  • have someone else holding their identification or documents
  • owe a debt to an employer or recruiter that never seems to get smaller
  • work very long hours for little or no pay, or live where they work
  • seem coached, or have someone else answer for them
  • be under 18 and exchanging sex for anything of value

No single sign proves trafficking, and many people who show these signs are not being trafficked. Several signs together are a reason to ask for advice.

If you suspect trafficking

  1. Do not confront anyone or try to intervene yourself. It can put the person, and you, in danger.
  2. Contact the National Human Trafficking Hotline at 1-888-373-7888 or text 233733. You can ask for advice, not only report.
  3. If a child is involved, report to the NCMEC CyberTipline at report.cybertip.org or call 1-800-843-5678.
  4. If anyone is in immediate danger, call 911.

Where the word comes from

“Traffic” originally meant trade, not transport. The word came into English in the early 1500s through Middle French trafique and Italian traffico, both meaning commerce. Today the word makes many people think of roads and movement, which is one reason the crime is misunderstood.

Child sexual exploitation

Any sexual abuse or exploitation of someone under 18, in person or online, including grooming and sextortion. It is most often committed by someone the child or their family knows and trusts. It often starts with slow trust-building, through attention, gifts and secrecy, and the child may not realize anything is wrong.

Warning signs

  • A new online friend, often claiming to be only slightly older, gives gifts such as game currency or gift cards.
  • They ask to move the conversation to a private app, or one where messages disappear.
  • They ask the child to keep the friendship secret from parents or friends.
  • The child becomes secretive about their phone or computer, or suddenly needs money or a bank card.
  • The child receives threats demanding images or money. This is called sextortion, and it is a crime against the child.

What to do

  1. Stay calm. A child who feels blamed is less likely to tell you more.
  2. Don’t delete the messages. Take screenshots if you can, then stop contact.
  3. In the U.S., report to the NCMEC CyberTipline at report.cybertip.org or 1-800-843-5678. Outside the U.S., find your national hotline through INHOPE.
  4. If a child is in immediate danger, call 911 or your local emergency number.

Talk early and often. Children are more likely to come to you if they already know you won’t take their phone away or blame them.

Elder financial exploitation

The illegal or improper use of an older adult’s money, property or assets, most often by someone they know and trust, such as a relative, caregiver or new friend. Scams by strangers are a major risk too.

Warning signs

  • Savings drop faster than expected, or there are withdrawals, transfers or purchases the person can’t explain.
  • A new person suddenly takes control of banking, bills or mail, or becomes power of attorney.
  • Sudden changes to a will, deed or beneficiary, especially in favor of someone new.
  • The older person seems anxious, isolated from friends or family, or says they aren’t allowed to talk about money.
  • They defend the person involved, or feel they “owe” them. Loyalty doesn’t rule out exploitation.

What to do

  1. Talk with the older person privately and calmly. Listen more than you speak.
  2. Ask their bank to review the account. Many banks have teams trained to spot exploitation.
  3. In the U.S., contact Adult Protective Services in their state, or the National Elder Fraud Hotline at 1-833-372-8311.
  4. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911 or your local emergency number.

Predatory scams

Schemes that trick people into handing over money or personal information themselves, through fake relationships, investments, emergencies or officials. They target people of every age. They work by building trust, fear, urgency or hope, then cutting you off from anyone who might question the story.

Warning signs

  • You are contacted out of the blue by someone claiming to be your bank, a government agency, a delivery company or tech support.
  • You are told to act right now, keep it secret, or that you will be arrested or lose money if you hang up.
  • You are asked to pay by gift card, wire transfer or cryptocurrency, or to move money to a “safe account.”
  • Someone you met online, but never properly on video, steers you toward an investment platform or asks for money in an emergency.
  • An investment promises high, steady returns, and lets you withdraw a little at first to build your trust.

What to do

  1. Stop. Hang up or stop replying. A real organization will give you time to check.
  2. Contact the organization yourself, using the number on your card, bill or official website, not the one you were given.
  3. If you paid, call your bank or card provider immediately. Fast action sometimes stops a transfer.
  4. Report it: in the U.S. at reportfraud.ftc.gov and, for online crime, ic3.gov. For cross-border scams, use econsumer.gov.

Shame keeps scams going. People who are scammed often don’t tell anyone. If it happens to someone you know, start with “this happens to careful people,” not “how could you?”
